## Problem

Generative AI technology was moving quickly into products, but knowledge of the models and criteria for evaluating them remained concentrated in a few specialist teams. The web team relied on AI LAB's interpretation when assessing or implementing new features. Nontechnical roles sometimes conflated even basic concepts such as training and inference.

This was more than a knowledge gap. Teams lacked the shared language and practical criteria to identify problems AI could solve in their own work, then assess feasibility and user value for themselves. The organization needed to move beyond one-off education and give teams a way to define and test problems independently.

## Approach

My goal was to help each team form the right questions and make decisions in its own work, rather than pack the sessions with information. I began by identifying the decisions each audience actually had to make and the points where it relied on an AI specialist team. I then recast the same technology in language suited to each role and working context.

I covered training and inference with nontechnical roles, R2V's reference structure and implementation boundaries with the web team, and Prompt Skills and reliable sources with the creative team. AI LAB and the web team received separate Competitive Audits for evaluating markets and models. I shared product direction and the 3D control strategy at different levels of depth with C-level and technical audiences.

I expanded the sessions into a Product Discovery program where the web team identifies user friction directly and tests whether AI can address it.

## Execution

### Shared language and practical foundations, 4 sessions

I ran two AI 101 sessions for nontechnical roles, a conceptual and hands-on R2V session for web developers, and a Prompt Skills briefing for the creative team. Beginning with accurate model terminology, each session provided concepts and criteria the audience could apply directly to its work.

### Criteria for evaluating markets and models, 3 sessions

I ran separate Competitive Audits for AI LAB and the web team. I also shared ways to apply Image Generation models with AI LAB. The focus was on criteria for evaluating adoption against product problems, rather than treating every new model as another list of features.

### Aligning product direction and technical vision, 6 sessions

For technical audiences, I shared a strategy for controlling models with 3D. I explained the product vision before and after the pivot to C-level and technical audiences at different levels of depth. The sessions put four topics on a shared organizational agenda: the strengths of the existing 3D product, its expressive limits, the roles to hand over to generative models, and the areas where 3D should remain the focus.

### From Sessions to Discovery

To turn the shared language from the sessions into practical work, I started a web-team-led Product Discovery program. The web team identifies user friction, selects problems AI could solve, then prototypes and tests them quickly. The program is still in its early operating stage as the organization works to broaden its problem-solving capability.

## Results

- **INDEPENDENT R2V DELIVERY**: Using concepts and implementation criteria shared in advance, the web team implemented the R2V feature without relying on AI LAB.
- **WEB-LED AI DISCOVERY**: The web team began identifying new feature candidates at the intersection of the product and AI, then evaluating their feasibility on its own.
- **SHARED AI LANGUAGE**: Distinctions between AI concepts that had been conflated, such as training and inference, became clearer across the organization. Teams could discuss technology and product in more precise language.
