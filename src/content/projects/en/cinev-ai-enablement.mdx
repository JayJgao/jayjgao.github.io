## Problem

Generative AI was moving quickly into the product, but knowledge of the models and the criteria for judging them sat with a small number of specialist teams. When the web team reviewed or implemented a new feature, it relied on AI LAB's interpretation. In nontechnical roles, even foundational concepts such as training and inference were sometimes used interchangeably.

Missing knowledge was only part of the problem. Teams did not share the language or working criteria they needed to spot problems AI could solve in their own work and judge feasibility and user value for themselves. The organization needed to move beyond one-off training and give teams a way to define and test problems independently.

## Approach

I focused on helping each team ask the right questions and make sound decisions in its own work. I first mapped the decisions each group had to make and the points where it depended on AI specialists. I then reframed the same technology in terms that fit each role and work context.

For nontechnical roles, I covered fundamentals such as training and inference. The web team received guidance on R2V's reference structure and implementation boundaries, while the creative team learned Prompt Skills and how to identify reliable sources of information. AI LAB and the web team each received a Competitive Audit for evaluating the market and models. I discussed product direction and 3D control strategy with C-level and technical teams at different levels of depth.

I extended the work beyond standalone sessions into a Product Discovery program in which the web team finds user friction directly and tests whether AI can address it.

## Execution

The work comprised exactly 13 AI Literacy and visioning sessions, grouped into three streams.

### Shared language and practical foundations, 4 sessions

I ran two AI 101 sessions for nontechnical roles, one session on R2V concepts and hands-on practice for web developers, and one Prompt Skills briefing for the creative team. We started by naming models accurately, then built concepts and decision criteria each group could use in its day-to-day work.

### Criteria for evaluating markets and models, 3 sessions

I ran one Competitive Audit for AI LAB and one for the web team, then held a third session with AI LAB on ways to use Image Generation models. The focus was on assessing adoption value against product problems instead of treating each new model as another list of features.

### Aligning product direction and technical vision, 6 sessions

I shared a model control strategy using 3D with technical teams. I also explained the product vision before and after the pivot to C-level and technical groups at different levels of depth. The strengths and expressive limits of the existing 3D product, what generative models should take over, and where 3D should focus became topics for the organization to address together.

### From Sessions to Discovery

To put the shared language from the sessions into practice, I launched a Product Discovery program led by the web team. The team identifies user friction, selects problems that AI could solve, then prototypes and tests them quickly. The program is currently in an early stage, with the aim of broadening the organization's problem-solving capacity.

## Results

- **INDEPENDENT R2V DELIVERY**: Using the concepts and implementation criteria shared in advance, the web team implemented R2V without depending on AI LAB.
- **WEB-LED AI DISCOVERY**: The web team began identifying new feature candidates at the boundary between product and AI and taking the lead in assessing whether AI could solve the underlying problems.
- **SHARED AI LANGUAGE**: Across the organization, the distinction between AI concepts that had been used interchangeably, such as training and inference, became clearer. This made product and technical discussions more precise.
